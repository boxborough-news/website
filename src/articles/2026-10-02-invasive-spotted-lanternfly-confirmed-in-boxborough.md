On Monday, September 28, the town received notice from the Massachusetts Department of Agricultural Resources (MDAR) that the state had confirmed a spotted lanternfly infestation in Boxborough. The town posted a [notice](https://www.boxborough-ma.gov/m/newsflash/Home/Detail/623) the same day, and Town Administrator Michael Johns informed the Select Board that evening.

According to MDAR, the main host plant of the spotted lanternfly is the invasive tree of heaven, but the lanternfly also feeds on maple trees, birch trees, grapevines, and dozens of other plants. Although the invasive insects do not bite or sting, they gather in large numbers in late summer and fall and cover surfaces with a sticky, sugary waste called honeydew. MDAR biologist Dr. Jennifer Forman Orth notified the town that the state had received reports of lanternflies in the northwest part of Boxborough in late August. Surveyors investigated the sightings and found adult lanternflies as well as egg masses from a prior season. Forman Orth told Boxborough News that “the presence of old egg masses tells us that lanternfly has been there for at least two years.”

Later this season, MDAR or U.S. Department of Agriculture staff will check trees to map the extent of the infestation. Residents can [report lanternfly sightings at a state website](https://massnrc.org/pests/slfreport.aspx). Based on observations of the lanternfly’s spread in other areas, MDAR says that eradication is impossible, and the focus is on slowing its proliferation.

A recent [MDAR press release](https://www.mass.gov/news/state-agricultural-officials-urge-residents-to-help-limit-spread-of-spotted-lanternfly-across-massachusetts) advises three steps for residents and businesses to take. First, motorists “should inspect their vehicles before leaving an infested area to avoid accidentally transporting spotted lanternflies to new locations.” Second, people can “manage spotted lanternflies by vacuuming up the insects, removing preferred host plants such as trees of heaven, or setting up targeted traps.” Third, “residents and business owners should keep a close watch for spotted lanternfly egg masses during the fall and winter months and scrape off and destroy any that are within reach.”

“We are planning to follow the recommendations from the state exactly, as it seems public education, awareness, and individual action on a large scale is the best way to curb this invasive insect,” Johns said.
