Kate Crosby, who is retiring at the end of the school year after more than 15 years as the Acton-Boxborough Regional School District’s (ABRSD) energy manager, was honored at the School Committee’s September 17 business meeting.

Facilities Manager Marc Hamel publicly thanked Crosby for her years of service and leadership. “Over the years, Kate has been a driving force behind our efforts to make our schools more energy efficient, sustainable, and environmentally responsible,” Hamel said. “Her work has had an impact not only on our facilities but also on the culture and values of the entire district. She brought knowledge, enthusiasm, and determination to this work and never hesitated to champion an idea that she believed would make a difference. The work she has done will continue to benefit our District long after her retirement.”

Acton-Boxborough’s long history of integrating energy efficiency and waste reduction with academic learning and health and wellness is due to Crosby who, since beginning her tenure in 2010-11, has engaged the entire K-12 school community in conservation advocacy, starting with a District-wide initiative called [Power Down](https://www.usgbc.org/sites/default/files/2022-06/Behavior-based-Efficiency.pdf) which resulted in significant, ongoing savings for the District.

Hamel noted that “Crosby’s leadership and persistence led to Acton’s recognition by Mass Save as a Climate Leader, and she secured $1.5M from the Mass Clean Energy Center for electric school bus deployment along with an additional $200,000 from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency’s Clean School Bus Program. Those investments will benefit our students and community for many years to come.”

In her role as advisor to Resource Force, a student organization at Acton-Boxborough Regional High School, Crosby supported students in successfully advocating for School Committee adoption of a zero emissions vehicle [policy](https://abschools.community.diligentoneplatform.com/home/policies/policydoc/4dc28010-1831-43bb-be4b-050ead8cddbd) in 2024.

Crosby was substantially involved in design efforts related to the all-electric, geothermal, net-zero-ready Boardwalk Campus, an Acton elementary school building which opened in 2022 and for which Crosby was named Change Agent of the Year in 2024 by the Massachusetts’ chapter of the U.S. Green Building Council. Superintendent of Schools Peter Light told the School Committee that it was Crosby who secured a $2.9M federal rebate for the construction of the building’s geothermal well system.

During her remarks, Crosby told the School Committee that “positions like this save hundreds of thousands of dollars each year in energy costs, and we’ve raked in many more millions in tax credits, rebates, and incentives. I am grateful the District has recognized the value of that and is going to maintain this position when I depart.”

Jillian Makia will be assuming the energy manager position in Acton-Boxborough next year and is currently working with Crosby to transition into the role.

### Elementary PTO leaders recognized for community building work at a critical time

Leaders of the newly formed elementary Parent-Teacher Organizations (PTOs) were recognized at the September 17 School Committee meeting for their strong community-building work over the summer and ongoing efforts to support a smooth transition as the newly reorganized elementary schools welcomed over 2000 elementary students back to school this fall.

Superintendent of Schools Peter Light commended the PTO transition team for coordinating an unprecedented four to five meet-and-greets for families at every grade level at each school over the summer and for their behind-the-scenes, detailed legal work to formally close the former elementary PTOs and reopen them under a new school organizational structure. “These are volunteers doing their best by our kids and staff,” Light said. “They put in an incredible amount of work and time to get organized and get going.”

Melissa Cottone, the new PTO Publicity Coordinator for the Parker-Damon Building Upper School who previously served as PTO President at the now closed Conant Elementary School, provided a brief update on the transition. “Its been a long, collaborative, and thoughtful process. The good news is that the [Parker-Damon Upper and Lower School PTO](https://www.parkerdamonpto.org/) and the [Boardwalk Campus Upper and Lower School PTO](https://www.boardwalkpto.org/) are up-and-running. This team has been amazing. We had a great start to the school year supporting students, faculty, and families. From day-one we had a sense of community and belonging.”

Lacey Johnson, the new PTO co-chair at the Boardwalk Campus Lower School said, “Behind all the bylaws, budgets, and bank accounts, what has been really special to see is how all the PTOs came together to share resources and how many people have stepped up to make students and staff across the District feel welcome and supported. We organized summer playground meet-ups so kids could start making connections and get comfortable in their new environments, we stocked staff lounges to welcome teachers and staff, and we started planning fall events to bring our communities together. Our PTOs are ready to fundraise and support our schools.”

Johnson told the Committee, “We could not have done this without the support and collaboration of our principals. Their willingness to partner with us has made a huge difference. The work has been easier than anticipated, and I credit that to the work that happened before our kids ever walked in the doors. After a difficult year of change and uncertainty, that feels worth celebrating.”

Other members of the PTO transition team in attendance were also acknowledged at the September 17 meeting and included Betsy Adalsteinsson, PTO co-chair of the Parker-Damon Lower School; Melissa Elmore and Soula Stratakias, who are currently serving as Enrichment Coordinators at the Parker-Damon Lower and Upper Schools, respectively; and Mollie Kaufman, PTO co-chair of the Parker-Damon Building Upper School.

### Families press for additional recess time for elementary students

The benefits recess can have on physical health, brain function, and social development are well-documented, but how much is enough?

The District’s current [procedures](https://abschools.community.diligentoneplatform.com/home/policies/policydoc/77232ef1-24c7-48e4-a57a-97158fe9412e) direct all elementary schools to have at least 20 minutes a day of supervised recess, a time span that concurs with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommendation. The Massachusetts’ Department of Elementary and Secondary Education (DESE) has no statewide recess requirement.

Assistant Superintendent for Teaching and Learning Gabby Abrams reported at the September 17 School Committee meeting that Acton-Boxborough families are pressing for additional recess time for elementary-age children.

Abrams told the School Committee that, currently in Acton-Boxborough, students in kindergarten and grade one have two 20-minute recess periods daily while students in grades 2-6 have 25-minutes of recess each day. “We’ve had a lot of questions from families about adding a recess period for students in grades 2-6,” Abrams said.

“It’s really important that we hear the experiences of our families, especially as elementary school reorganization is implemented,” Abrams noted. “We agree students need unstructured opportunities to explore, discover, self-regulate and socialize. That time is sacrosanct within our schedule and guaranteed for students. The ‘how’ [to add more recess time] is always the sticky wicket. If we add a second recess, we’d need to find more structured learning time somewhere else in order to meet state requirements for time-on-learning. The state requires us to provide 900 hours of structured learning time at the elementary schools. Recess does not count toward structured learning time.”

Boxborough School Committee Member Hilary Greven asked about the possibility of increasing outdoor learning as part of structured learning time which, according to Abrams, is currently part of the science curriculum. “That’s an area we can talk more about,” responded Abrams.

Abrams informed the Committee that state legislators have proposed an [amendment](https://malegislature.gov/Search?SearchTerms=recess+3) currently under consideration in the state House of Representatives that requests 30 consecutive minutes of recess for students in grades K-8 that would also count toward student learning time.

### SEPAC addresses concerns about assistive technology

Acton’s Special Education Parent Advisory Council (SEPAC) Co-Chairs Amanda Bailey and Kristin O’Neil had an opportunity to present their fall update to the School Committee on September 17.

Priority areas for 2026-27 include building relationships with new District staff which includes the District’s new assistant director of special education, Allison Leahy, and updating SEPAC’s [dyslexia guidelines](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1b-h2DkyMvSIi3u79yEFAGp4cy9mCqOlg/view). SEPAC will also continue to monitor the AB Forward implementation progress at the newly reorganized elementary schools specific to students with disabilities and have been invited to serve on the upcoming Superintendent Search Committee.

Bailey and O’Neil’s update included an important discussion on student use of assistive technology (AT). “We are seeing incidents as policies and procedures are put into place in schools around device use, bans and restrictions, that considerations around AT use by special education students are not always accounted for.”

Bailey explained that assistive technology is defined as any item, equipment, or product that is utilized to increase, maintain, or improve functional capabilities for a child with a disability and can include use of pencil grips, screen readers, audio books, talk-to-text software, spellcheckers, noise cancelling headphones, calculators, or apps, etc. in the context of special education.

“We are telling parents to communicate any concerns around access to AT with their special education team,” Bailey said. Bailey and O’Neil also requested that the School Committee consider strengthening current policy language related to AT and publish it in student handbooks for ease of access by parents, students and staff.

During the discussion period, School Committee Member Dennis Burianek asked Bailey how SEPAC determines if this problem has become systemic. Bailey responded that the SEPAC is “including a question about AT in an upcoming survey to determine the scope of the concerns and are talking with staff and families.”

Members Ben Bloomenthal and Glen Cote voiced concern around the stigma around AT use by students with disabilities and particularly in classroom contexts when use of tech is restricted. Bailey responded, “It comes down to good communication. Any time a broad restriction is put into place, we lose the “I” in IEP. (IEP refers to an Individualized Education Plan, a required legal document for eligible students with documented disabilities that may include information on AT use.) “If a parent hears that their child is struggling with an assignment due to AI fears or were made to hand-write an essay in class where normally they would’ve had access to AT, we need to start those conversations immediately.”

Member Andrew Schwarz asked what frameworks are available to help the School Committee with policy development on AT. In response, Bailey referred to a 2024 [publication](https://sites.ed.gov/idea/files/Myths-and-Facts-Surrounding-Assistive-Technology-Devices-01-22-2024.pdf) by the U.S. Department of Education entitled Myths and Facts Surrounding Assistive Technology Devices and Services.

In response to a query by School Committee Chair Tori Campbell regarding how to best help Acton-Boxborough educators and students around special education students’ use of AT, Natalia Kierul, the director of special education for the district, asked a few rhetorical questions that spoke to the larger issue of school climate and culture: “How do we build a community where differences are appreciated and where students feel appreciated and understood? How can educators collaborate and design lessons that are accessible to all learners?” Kierul also underscored the importance of good communication “so that classroom teachers, families, and specialists are on the same page.” Superintendent of Schools Peter Light concurred: “I think what Amanda is talking about is making sure individuals who require exceptions know we’re listening and recognize it up front so it’s not always just about self-advocacy.”
