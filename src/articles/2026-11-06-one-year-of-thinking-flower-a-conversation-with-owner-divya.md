As Thinking Flower celebrates its first anniversary, owner and Boxborough resident Divya Suryaprasad reflects on transforming a vacant commercial space into a thriving bakery, the community that has embraced her entrepreneurialism, and the challenges and rewards of building a business from scratch. This interview has been edited for clarity and brevity.

**Boxborough News (BN): Congratulations on Thinking Flower's first anniversary\! Looking back on your first year, what would you like to communicate to the community?**

Divya (D): I was uncertain and worried going into this business. Starting a food establishment is very expensive. There's so much capital involved, from equipment to construction, and once you're in, you're in. There's no backing out. I was especially worried because this was my first venture opening my own coffee shop. I'd always worked under other chefs, and you don't carry the same stress when you're working for someone else. The town of Boxborough has just blown us away. We're such a small town, with relatively few families, and we're not on a busy commuting road. But I think I've proved a point: if you make a good product, people will come, no matter where you are.

**BN: You received a grant from Boxborough's Economic Development Committee to launch Thinking Flower. What was the significance of that support?**

D: I appreciated how excited they were about the whole idea. They were so excited to see this business come to life. I put the initial funding toward equipment and construction, but their support went far beyond the financial side. There are so many permits involved, from the Fire Department to the Board of Health. I was so naive about how much paperwork I would need to do\! But everybody came together and helped me navigate the process. They wanted this business to succeed. My landlord, the town—everybody played a part. It takes a village. I don't think I could have done this without their support.

**BN: As you approach your first anniversary, how have production, staffing, and customer demand evolved?**

D: When we first opened, I was absolutely freaking out about the size of our pastry case. I thought, "I cannot make enough croissants to fill this case\! What am I going to do?" I considered filling some of the space with jams or merchandise. Now I think the case is too small.  We started with about ten varieties of pastries and now sometimes have as many as twenty-five, especially on Saturdays. We initially made around 150 croissants a day. Now we're producing approximately 300 to 350 daily, sometimes more on busy production days. Across our croissants, cakes, cookies, and other baked goods, we can prepare around 400 to 500 items on a busy day. We've also grown our team to eight people, including me, with a combination of full-time and part-time employees. We're continually hiring as demand increases. We're working six days a week on production, even though we're not open to customers every day. Sometimes I'm here on Sundays, too, just to catch up\! We're approaching the limits of what our current kitchen and equipment can accommodate. That's a good problem to have, but it means we need to be thoughtful about how we grow.

**BN: What's been your greatest challenge as a first-time business owner?**

D: The biggest challenge is running the business itself. Making croissants and coffee comes naturally to me. But managing inventory, staffing, ordering, planning menus, and everything else has been a huge learning experience. I'm not naturally a very organized person, and I'm impulsive. I've had to change so much. Another challenge is learning to delegate. I'm very particular about how things look. I'll start showing someone how to frost a muffin, and before I know it, I've frosted all twenty-four myself\! My employees remind me that I have to let them practice. I'm learning to step back and trust my team. That's the only way the business can continue to grow.

**BN: How do you determine projected sales so that you don't have too much left over, but also produce enough to meet demand? How do you make that calculation?**

D: I feel like there's no way we can make it exact. It is hard. Luckily, the point-of-sale software we use tells us how many items we sell on a typical Tuesday, Wednesday, and so on. But that only helps a little because there are days like this when weather plays a huge role. Interestingly, when it's gloomy outside, everybody likes to hang out inside with a nice cup of coffee and a pastry. So when it's raining, we actually get more foot traffic. When it's a beautiful, bright day, nobody wants to come in\! We have set numbers that we know we'll usually sell. But there are days when we sell out by noon. There are days when you come in at eleven and we don't have any ham-and-cheese or spinach-and-feta croissants left. It's impossible to know exactly how many to make. The unfortunate thing is that we bake everything the morning of, and we don't serve those same pastries the next day.

**BN: Looking ahead, are there plans to expand your hours, introduce alcohol service, or open another location?**

D: We have our liquor license, and my original vision included wine by the glass and perhaps mimosas with breakfast. But serving alcohol comes with additional responsibilities, and I want to make sure we have the right team and training in place. We're hiring baristas, bakers, and front-of-house staff. Eventually, I want to start opening Sundays. That's definitely something I see happening in the near future. I'd love to eventually operate seven days a week. But we need enough staff and equipment to support that. I'm not considering a second location. I'd rather expand our production capacity and focus on maintaining quality here. I've learned that it's okay to do things slowly and in phases.

**BN: What are some of the customer favorites? And your favorite menu item?**

D: Our pistachio plum jam croissant is a hit\! When we introduced it, we'd make sixty just on Saturdays, and they would still sell out. Ham-and-cheese is always a bestseller, and almond croissants are another favorite. But I love encouraging people to try something new. We have chocolate hazelnut, pistachio rose, and seasonal varieties. My personal favorite is the cardamom custard bun. Growing up in India, cardamom was everywhere. It's like cinnamon for Americans. When customers ask what it tastes like, we explain that it's one of the spices in a chai latte. It's a unique flavor, and people have really embraced it. If there's one left at the end of the day, I'm very happy to take it home\!

**BN: You've spoken about the value of neighborhood gathering places. Was owning a bakery always your ambition?**

D: Even in my early twenties, I knew I wanted to establish a bakery or coffee shop. What I enjoy most is taking care of people and feeding people. I have a bachelor's degree in computer science and worked at Oracle in India for two years. But I really didn't belong there\! Eventually, I studied baking and pastry arts at Johnson & Wales University. I worked under different chefs around Boston, learning the craft and observing how businesses operated. When I got into the science behind roasting coffee and extracting espresso, I realized that a good cup of coffee and a good pastry belonged together. Those two things were nonnegotiable for me. I learned about coffee, went to school for coffee in California, and became certified as a barista. I wanted to make sure everything came together in the best way I could. So opening a shop was always in my mind, but I was timid about becoming an entrepreneur. I didn't want to take on all those risks. I also have two kids, and my husband has a busy career. I was always reminded that if I opened my own shop, I didn't know whether I would be able to multitask and manage everything. So I parked that idea for many years. But it was always there. Whenever we traveled as a family, especially outside the country, if you looked back at our camera roll, everything was bakeries and coffee shops. That's where my heart was. I would say things like, “If I ever open one, I'm definitely going to incorporate this aspect of it.” I found so much inspiration in those places. The motivation to own a small, community-focused, neighborhood bakery and coffee shop was always there. I just never acted on it. Then one morning, my commute into Boston took two and a half hours. I couldn't finish my work because I had to leave to pick up my children, and I still didn't make it home in time. I thought, "It's just not worth my time." If I was spending three and a half hours commuting instead of making croissants, I might as well believe I could open my own shop. Now I live four minutes away\!

**BN: Finally, what does reaching this first anniversary mean to you?**

D: I hadn't even thought about it until recently. Suddenly I realized, "Oh my God, we did it\! We survived the first year\!" It's been an amazing journey, and we've learned so much. Of course, a business has to be financially successful. But I believe that if you make a good product, provide good service, and maintain quality, success will naturally follow. Most of all, I want to say thank you to Boxborough. The support from this community has meant everything to us.
