At its meeting on June 5, the Finance Committee (FinCom) met with Town Assessor Kelly Szocik to address questions asked at Annual Town Meeting (ATM) with respect to the amount of annual funding requested for cyclical property inspections and certification compliance, as well as the “overlay reserve,” a fund to cover potential liabilities related to tax abatements, exemptions, and uncollectible tax bills. The Massachusetts Department of Revenue (DOR) requires inspections of both residential and commercial real estate property every ten years and personal property inspections every five years.

At Annual Town Meeting in 2021, the town voted $120,000 to ensure sufficient funds were available to complete the required inspections over a period of time. Additional funding of $30,000 per year was voted at ATM 2022, 2023, 2024 and 2025. Szocik said that despite having funding available in 2021, turnover in the Assessor’s office and the COVID pandemic delayed the inspection cycles. According to Szocik, at the time of her hire in July 2022, there was a backlog of approximately 1,800 parcels that needed to be inspected. A consulting firm was hired to complete the inspections at an average annual cost of approximately $35,000 over a 3-year period.

As of April 2025, the initial warrant article funding from ATM 2021 had essentially been depleted; however, the additional funding from subsequent years (2022 through 2025) had created a new balance of $120,000 available for inspections. Szocik said that “Boxborough enters FY2026 in a maintenance phase, ahead of pace,” which means that the town is essentially caught up with inspections. Szocik has contracted with a consulting firm to complete 500 more inspections in FY2026 at a projected cost of $13,500. However, Szocik projected annual costs for inspections between $20,000 and $40,000. She did not explain the difference between the consulting contract and her projections.

FinCom members asked if the DOR required a certain reserve level to fund future evaluations, or given the current “buffer” of $120,000, could the town “pause” the funding? Szocik said she did not know the answer to that question and would consult with the DOR. Turning their discussion to the Town’s property tax revenue and the funding required to cover liabilities from abatements, exemptions, and uncollectible tax bills, FinCom discussed the “overlay reserve,” which is a dedicated fund set aside to cover those potential exposures. Mass General Law provides that the assessor determines the amount needed for the overlay reserve; the reserve does not require town meeting approval for appropriation.

Szocik projected current liabilities at $1.75M, and FinCom members voiced their disagreement with her calculation of the liabilities. FinCom believes that the reserve amount could be reduced. When asked for comment after the meeting, FinCom Chair Tony Newton said, “FinCom did not receive satisfactory answers around either the cost and process for revaluations or management of the overlay reserve and will continue to probe both.” The property tax abatement is a way for property owners to dispute their tax bill if they believe their property has been overvalued or improperly assessed. If the assessor determines that they are owed an abatement, that expected revenue becomes a liability or “exposure.” The assessor evaluates the potential exposures from abatements and exemptions of property taxes and sets aside funding to cover those exposures in a dedicated account called the “overlay reserve.”

The overlay reserve is calculated based on a five-year average of abatements, exemptions, uncollectibles, and Appellate Tax Board (ATB) exposure(s). The ATB is a state board that hears and decides state and local tax appeals, including appeals related to property tax; if a resident files for an abatement and the assessor denies the abatement, they can then file with the Appellate Tax Board. In other matters, FinCom members briefly discussed continuing problems with ClearGov, the software program that provides budgeting data for the town. The budget data is uploaded by the town accountant and then made available in ClearGov for reporting. FinCom members recounted their experience during the last budgeting cycle; hundreds of hours were spent manipulating data from the ClearGov system to create acceptable reports to analyze trends and present a budget at ATM.

Some FinCom members expressed concern that the current system isn’t efficient and doesn’t provide clear and transparent details of individual budgets. They also agreed that the town may need to explore a process that doesn’t rely on volunteers to create acceptable reports. There was also a discussion about the reduction in the Planning Board line item for consulting services and the ensuing debate that transpired on town meeting floor. FinCom reiterated that no member of FinCom has access to individual budgets in ClearGov and, therefore, cannot make any changes to budgets. Those changes can only be made by town administration.

When reached for comment, Town Administrator Michael Johns said, "In general, I communicate with department heads prior to cutting any budget lines. Seeing that none of the appropriation from the FY25 budget had been spent, it seemed fiscally prudent to cut the consulting line back in FY26. If I missed communicating this with the department head, it was an oversight.” FinCom members said they would need to explore methods and processes to ensure this doesn’t happen again. The next FinCom meeting is scheduled for Tuesday June 24 at 7 pm. The meeting is hybrid.
