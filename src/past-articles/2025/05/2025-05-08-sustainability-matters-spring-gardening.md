Spring is here, and many of us are thinking about gardening. Reasons for gardening sustainably include supporting our local ecosystem, conserving water, fighting climate change and saving money. Consider some of these ideas to make your garden more sustainable this year.

Use of plants native to our area can support the local ecosystem. Some species need a specific plant to complete their life cycle; for example, milkweed is an important host plant for monarch butterflies. Local nurseries often have native plant sections. It isn’t necessary to eliminate non-native plants you may already have, as long as they aren’t invasive, but if you are looking to fill in a spot in your garden, or are thinking of creating a new bed, consider native plants that would be suitable for the growing conditions in your yard. Native plants will also require less water once they are established.

Other ways to minimize water usage beyond selecting drought tolerant plants include collecting water in rain barrels and watering only when needed, preferably early in the morning or late in the day to minimize evaporation. Mulching your garden beds will help to retain moisture as well as adding organic material back into the soil.

Minimizing the area of your lawn by replacing it with native plants is another sustainable garden option. Lawns require water to maintain, and offer little habitat to native species**.** It’s not necessary to completely eliminate your lawn; you can extend an existing garden bed or add an island garden.

Sheet mulching is one way to eliminate lawn and create more garden space; this involves smothering your lawn and adding nutrients to the soil. It may take 1-3 months for your new garden bed to be ready for planting.

The first step after deciding where your new garden will be located is to aerate it, then cover with leaves or leaf mulch. Place plain cardboard on top of the leaf mulch; if you are using shipping boxes, be sure to remove all tape and labels. Make sure pieces of cardboard overlap, so no grass grows in between. Then soak the cardboard with water and cover with 4 to 6 inches of compost and mulch.

Reconsider your use of fertilizer on your property and get a soil test before adding any fertilizer to your lawn or garden. You can send a sample of your soil to UMass for testing; more information can be found at this UMass Amherst website: [https://bit.ly/42PVF9i](https://bit.ly/42PVF9i).

If you choose to fertilize, it is best to use organic fertilizers. Chemical fertilizers are made from synthetic materials. Runoff will pollute water sources and decrease beneficial organisms in soil, and buildup of chemical fertilizer can damage plant roots. Organic fertilizers, including your own homemade or purchased compost, are non-toxic, less likely to contribute to water pollution and take longer to break down, releasing nutrients into soil over time as plants need them.

While larger systemic changes are needed to address the environmental challenges we face, such as climate change, even small steps like those described here can help.
