This time of year, we anxiously await the arrival of new birds as they make their way north to their breeding grounds. One of these arrivals is awaited with worried conversations and predictions, “Have you seen a Hummingbird yet?”

The answer for Boxborough is, “Yes, they are here and more are moving through daily.” The individuals we see today are probably migrating further north and our resident birds will be here soon.

Many put up feeders and watch them throughout the summer. We see them argue and fight around the territory of the feeder with open aggression and control. The acrobatic skills amaze us. We watch the males arguing for territory, the females looking for nest sites, and later, the babies learning how to be Hummingbirds. We are entertained for hours.

With that in mind, what will the newly arrived birds find? How successful will they continue to be here?

Massachusetts has only one species of Hummingbird, the Ruby-Throated. Aptly named by the male's flashy, colorful throat patch. But did you know that most of their food and energy source comes from eating insects? The babies grow exclusively on bugs for protein; it is only when they fledge that they will find nectar from flowers and our sugary feeder. If Hummingbirds lose the insect food source, they will fail in successfully raising their babies.

Where do the insects come from that feed and nourish the adults and babies? Your stately Oak trees are the number one source. Oak trees host 450 different insect species, eggs, larvae or adults. These insects feed many species beside the Hummingbird. Warblers, Flycatchers, Phoebes, Swallows, Thrushes and Mockingbirds to name a few.

Other deciduous trees are hosts to various insects, but none rival the sheer number an old Oak tree can provide. If we cut down the Oaks, we lose the food source of many birds and they will not be successful in raising young. Displaced birds cannot simply move to another area of standing trees because that area is already taken by birds who defend their territory because their life and future depend on it. Over a short period of time the population dies out and we walk around wondering why the woods are quieter each year.

Overall, birds need quality food sources, quality nesting sites (in living trees amongst the branches and in dead trees in the holes they can excavate in the wood) and safe places away from cats and dogs. We are not helpless in this scenario, and there are many choices we can make to help the animals that frequent our yards. We need to preserve and protect their nesting and food resources and be better stewards of our yards and properties. If we change the habitat, we lose the species.
