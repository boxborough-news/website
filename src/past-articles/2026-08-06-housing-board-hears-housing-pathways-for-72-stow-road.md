At its meeting on Tuesday, July 28, the Boxborough Housing Board heard a forty-minute presentation from Associate Town Planner Ian Gilson outlining six zoning and permitting options to consider for the the dispensation of the 70-72 Stow Road property, which was purchased by the Town in 2010 to build an affordable housing project, but has remained undeveloped.

Before launching into his presentation, Gilson acknowledged the difference between what he was presenting and what the board heard in the May 2026 presentation by Housing Board member Joe Ferguson. Gilson described Ferguson’s presentation as “big idea options…of sell versus keep and develop,” whereas his (Gilson’s) presentation focused only on rezoning options and “trying to answer the technical questions that the board may have in regards to rezoning this lot.”

Zoning options presented ranged from a “site specific overlay district” to adding the parcel to the MBTA Communities overlay district to using one of the state’s programs under various sections of Massachusetts General Law Chapter 40, including Chapter 40A, 40B, 40R, and 40Y.

Chapter 40A is the section of Massachusetts General Law that “gives cities and towns authority to adopt ordinances and bylaws to regulate the use of land, buildings and structures.” Under this approach, the Stow Road parcel would be rezoned to permit multifamily housing (the current zoning is Ag-Res, which does not permit multifamily units). This would require a two-thirds vote at Town Meeting.

While Chapter 40A establishes standard local control over land use, Chapters 40B, 40R, and 40Y act as specific mechanisms designed to bypass or modify local zoning rules to increase the state's affordable housing supply.

Gilson noted how each program and zoning option works, what types of housing could be built, and what the “tradeoffs for Boxborough” would be under each option.

Chapter 40B, sometimes referred to as the “Anti-Snob Zoning Act” or the “Comprehensive Permit Statute” deals with affordable housing. Under Chapter 40B, if a town’s housing stock is less than 10% affordable, developers can bypass local zoning bylaws—such as density limits or setbacks—to build large-scale housing, provided 20% to 25% of the units are affordable. However, since Boxborough’s affordable housing stock is greater than 10%, the zoning option Gilson presented was for a “Friendly 40B,” which would allow the town to select a development partner and negotiate an affordable project without rezoning. This would require approval from the Zoning Board of Appeals.

Chapter 40R, or “smart growth housing,” is a state program that encourages towns to adopt a “smart growth zoning overlay district,” to allow dense, multi-family, mixed-income housing by right. Projects must ensure that at least 20% of the units are affordable. Towns receive cash payments and building bonuses from the state. Gilson’s presentation indicated that the density requirement for this program “appears difficult under existing private water and wastewater conditions.”

Chapter 40Y is a relatively new, state-approved homeownership program that establishes a starter home zoning district. According to Gilson’s presentation, the zoning “permits starter homes by right at four or more homes per developable acre.” One of the tradeoffs for Boxborough may be that the number of bedrooms required may conflict with the wastewater capacity of the parcel. The program is administered by the Executive Office of Housing and Living Communities (EOHLC) and provides financial incentives to towns who participate.

Following the presentation, Housing Board Chair Al Murphy mentioned the legal requirements and constraints on the property, noting that the deed requires that the property be used for low and moderate income housing. There was no further discussion or action following the presentation.
